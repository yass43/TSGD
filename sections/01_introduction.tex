\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Stochastic gradient descent (SGD) is a cornerstone of stochastic
optimization and a workhorse of modern machine learning
\citep{bottou2018optimization}. It replaces the exact gradient with a
fresh stochastic estimate, making optimization possible when evaluating
the full gradient is expensive or infeasible. Its simplicity and
scalability are supported by a substantial convergence theory, including
guarantees for smooth nonconvex objectives
\citep{ghadimi2013stochastic}.

Fresh estimation is one fundamental way to construct a gradient estimate.
Momentum SGD (MSGD) adds another: it averages stochastic-gradient
information across iterations, reducing noise while potentially lagging
behind a changing gradient. A third mechanism is equally natural:
estimate how the gradient changes, and add that change to an existing
estimate. We call this mechanism \emph{Transported SGD (TSGD)}.
SGD estimates gradients afresh; TSGD estimates gradient changes and
transports an initial estimate along the optimization trajectory.

The motivation for transport is that estimating a change can be much
easier than estimating an absolute quantity. Two stochastic gradients
evaluated with the same sample can share substantial noise. Subtracting
them cancels the shared component, so their difference can accurately
estimate the true gradient change even when each gradient is highly
noisy. Transport exploits this information directly. For example,
additive noise cancels exactly from a shared-sample difference;
with an exact initial estimate, TSGD then follows the deterministic
gradient-descent trajectory, regardless of the additive noise variance.

This distinction changes which properties of stochastic noise matter.
Classical SGD guarantees depend on its pointwise magnitude, commonly
bounded by a variance parameter $\sigma^2$. TSGD instead depends on its
variation along the trajectory, measured by a similarity parameter
$\delta$, together with the accuracy of its initial estimate.
High similarity means \emph{small} $\delta$: stochastic gradient changes
closely match deterministic gradient changes. At fixed $\delta$ and
initialization accuracy, the TSGD guarantee is independent of $\sigma$.
Transport is therefore robust to large pointwise noise when the gradient
changes remain accurate. This principle applies to both finite-sum and
population objectives. Pure transport nevertheless preserves initialization
errors and accumulates errors in subsequent differences.

The transport recursion is already present in SARAH and inexact SARAH
\citep{nguyen2017sarah,nguyen2021inexact}, the path-integrated estimator
of SPIDER \citep{fang2018spider}, and the transport branch of PAGE
\citep{li2021page}. Independent inner-run analyses include SARAH-IN,
iSARAH-IN, and PAGE-NS
\citep{nguyen2017sarah_nonconvex,nguyen2021inexact,sokolov2022nonconvex}.
Transport also enters the mixed estimators of STORM/MVR and MARS
\citep{cutkosky2019momentum,yuan2025mars}. These connections suggest
studying transport as an elementary estimation mechanism in its own
right. Our purpose is to explain and unify this known mechanism, making
its strengths and limitations as visible as those of fresh estimation
and momentum averaging.

The paper develops this viewpoint through three contributions:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Fundamental viewpoint.} We present TSGD alongside SGD and
MSGD as a basic way of using stochastic-gradient information, centered on
estimating changes rather than absolute gradients. This perspective
explains why accurate gradient differences can be useful even when fresh
gradients are highly noisy, and identifies transport within established
optimization methods.
\item \textbf{Theory and understanding.} We explain similarity, separate
initialization and transport errors, and give a finite-horizon guarantee
under conditional assumptions on the gradient-change increments.
\item \textbf{Comparison and connections.} We compare SGD and TSGD through
their noise assumptions, convergence guarantees, and computational costs.
Exact examples and a controlled experiment identify when small noise
variation favors transport and when accurate fresh gradients favor SGD.
We discuss reasons why modern optimizers may benefit from combining
these mechanisms with momentum averaging.
\end{itemize}
The examples precede the convergence analysis so that the role of each
assumption is visible before the theorem. Detailed proofs and additional
calculations are collected in the appendices.
