\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Stochastic gradient descent (SGD) estimates gradients afresh.
Transported SGD (TSGD) estimates gradient changes and uses them to
update an existing estimate. SGD is a cornerstone of stochastic
optimization and a workhorse of machine learning: replacing the exact
gradient with a stochastic estimate makes each update simple and
scalable \citep{bottou2018optimization}. TSGD follows an equally
natural principle: it adds estimated gradient changes to its current
estimate, transporting information along the optimization trajectory.

Estimating changes can be much easier than estimating absolute
quantities. Subtracting gradients evaluated with the same sample
cancels their shared noise component. In particular, shared additive
noise cancels exactly, giving the true gradient change even when
individual gradients are highly noisy. With exact initialization as
well, TSGD follows gradient descent path by path, regardless of the
noise magnitude.

The two methods therefore depend on different aspects of the noise.
Classical SGD guarantees depend on its pointwise magnitude, commonly
bounded by a variance parameter $\sigma^2$
\citep{ghadimi2013stochastic}. TSGD guarantees, on the other hand, depend on how
the noise varies between the coupled evaluations along the trajectory.
Centered $\delta$-similarity quantifies this variation: small $\delta$
means that stochastic gradient changes closely match deterministic
gradient changes. Thus, transport can remain accurate even when
pointwise noise is large. We stress that centered similarity applies
to population objectives as well as finite sums.

Two sources of error determine TSGD's accuracy: error in the initial
gradient estimate and error in the estimated gradient changes.
The initial error persists; new transport errors accumulate along the
trajectory. Accurate differences preserve a good initial
estimate, but cannot correct an inaccurate one.
The TSGD guarantee therefore depends on both $\delta$ and the accuracy
of the initial estimate. At fixed values of these quantities, it is
independent of $\sigma$, although the noise magnitude can affect the
cost of obtaining an accurate initial estimate.

Least-squares regression provides a concrete example. Label noise
enters the stochastic gradient $u(u^\top x-b)$, but cancels from the
shared-sample difference $uu^\top(x-y)$. It therefore affects fresh
gradient estimates and TSGD's initialization, but not its subsequent
transport increments (Section~\ref{subsec:understanding-similarity}).

The transport recursion is not new. It appears in established
variance-reduced algorithms such as SARAH, SPIDER, and PAGE
\citep{nguyen2017sarah,fang2018spider,li2021page}, and independent
single-run analyses already exist
\citep{nguyen2017sarah_nonconvex,nguyen2021inexact,sokolov2022nonconvex}.
It is commonly encountered as part of methods that combine transport
with refreshing or mixing. Our purpose is to study the mechanism
directly, making its strengths and limitations as explicit as those
of SGD.

Momentum SGD (MSGD) provides a third fundamental mechanism:
averaging fresh gradients with past estimates. Section~\ref{sec:algorithms-and-errors}
introduces all three; Section~\ref{sec:connections} discusses their
combination in modern optimizers.

The paper develops this viewpoint through three contributions:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Fundamental viewpoint.} We present TSGD alongside SGD as
a basic way of using stochastic-gradient information: estimating changes
rather than absolute gradients. Similarity explains when these changes
can remain accurate despite noisy individual gradients.
\item \textbf{Theory and understanding.} We explain similarity, separate
initialization and transport errors, and give a finite-horizon guarantee
under conditional assumptions on the gradient change increments.
Exact quadratic dynamics quantify their interaction and show how the
estimator can vanish while the true gradient does not.
\item \textbf{Comparison and connections.} We construct oracles with
identical pointwise gradient distributions but different transport
accuracy, and give exact examples showing when SGD or TSGD has the
smaller stationarity error. Least-squares experiments illustrate these
regimes and the effect of initialization. We compare convergence
guarantees and oracle costs, and identify how established optimizers
combine transport with fresh estimation and momentum averaging.
\end{itemize}
The examples precede the convergence analysis so that the role of each
assumption is visible before the theorem. Detailed proofs and additional
calculations appear in the appendices.
