\section{TSGD as a Building Block of Modern Optimization}
\label{sec:connections}

TSGD is useful when pointwise stochastic gradients are noisy but their
changes along the trajectory are accurate. With small $\delta$ and
accurate initialization, it can carry useful gradient information
forward through these low-variation changes, even when fresh absolute
gradients have large variance. Several established methods build on
this transport mechanism in different ways: SARAH and SPIDER restart
it periodically, Geom-SARAH randomizes the restart time, and PAGE,
STORM, and MARS combine it with fresh gradients.

We use a common notation for their estimator updates: $H_{k+1}$ denotes
a fresh unbiased estimate of $\nabla f(x_{k+1})$, possibly a minibatch
average or the exact gradient, and $D_{k+1}$ estimates
$\nabla f(x_{k+1})-\nabla f(x_k)$. For a stochastic gradient difference,
the two endpoints always share the same sample or minibatch. The
identities below concern the estimators; stepsizes, output rules, and
update geometries remain part of the respective algorithms.

\subsection{SARAH and SPIDER: Periodic Restarts}
\label{subsec:periodic-restarts}

SARAH initializes each inner loop with a full gradient and then applies
$g_{k+1}=g_k+D_{k+1}$ using stochastic gradient differences
\citep{nguyen2017sarah,nguyen2017sarah_nonconvex}. Thus SARAH-IN is a
transport phase with exact initialization. Inexact SARAH replaces the
full gradient by a sufficiently accurate minibatch estimate
\citep{nguyen2021inexact}. SPIDER uses the same recursive estimator
between periodic full-gradient or large-batch recomputations
\citep{fang2018spider}; its normalized or adaptive iterate updates are
additional features of the method.

Within a transport phase starting at iteration $s$, the error identity
\eqref{eq:transport-error-identity} reads
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:restart-error-window}
E_k=E_s+\sum_{t=s}^{k-1}\mathbb E[\|d_{t+1}\|^2].
\end{equation}
At a restart, the estimator is replaced by $H_s$. An exact gradient sets
$E_s=0$; an independent batch of size $B$ gives $E_s\leq\sigma^2/B$
under BV. Consequently, tracking errors accumulate only within the
current phase. Shorter phases limit this accumulation but require more
frequent recomputations. Restarting corrects the estimator at the current
point; it does not undo the effect of earlier errors on the iterates.

\subsection{Geom-SARAH: Random Restarts}
\label{subsec:random-restarts}

Geom-SARAH retains the SARAH transport recursion but samples the length
of each inner loop from a geometric distribution
\citep{horvath2022adaptivity}. Each epoch begins with a fresh batch
estimate, runs a random number of transport steps, and passes its final
point to the next epoch. Its batch sizes and expected epoch lengths may
change across epochs.

To express this mechanism, let $N_j$ be the number of inner steps in
epoch $j$, and parameterize its distribution by
\[
\mathbb P(N_j=n)=p_j(1-p_j)^n,\qquad n\geq0,
\qquad \mathbb E[N_j]=\frac{1-p_j}{p_j}.
\]
The memoryless property corresponds to a constant stopping probability
within each epoch. With initialization batch size $B_j$ and transport
batch size $b_j$, the expected number of pointwise gradient evaluations
is at most $B_j+2b_j\mathbb E[N_j]$. Longer phases amortize the initial
batch over more steps but allow errors to accumulate for longer;
shorter phases refresh more often. The geometric law controls the
expected duration, without imposing a deterministic maximum on it.
Geom-SARAH couples this randomization with batch-size schedules in its
convergence analysis.

\subsection{PAGE and MARINA: Probabilistic Refreshing}
\label{subsec:probabilistic-refreshing}

PAGE makes the refresh decision at each iteration
\citep{li2021page}. For a fixed probability $p$, its estimator can be
written as
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:page-refresh-transport}
g_{k+1}=c_{k+1}H_{k+1}
       +(1-c_{k+1})(g_k+D_{k+1}),
\qquad c_{k+1}\sim\operatorname{Bernoulli}(p).
\end{equation}
The refresh branch uses a fresh batch, while the transport branch
typically uses a smaller batch of coupled differences. The decision is
independent of the previous history and the new samples. With
$q_k=\mathbb E[\|d_{k+1}\|^2]$ as in Section~3 and
$v_{k+1}=\mathbb E[\|H_{k+1}-\nabla f(x_{k+1})\|^2]$, conditioning on
the two branches gives
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:refresh-error-recursion}
E_{k+1}=(1-p)(E_k+q_k)+p\,v_{k+1}.
\end{equation}
The inherited error is multiplied by $1-p$, and each transport error
survives only until the next refresh. An exact refresh has $v_{k+1}=0$;
a stochastic refresh replaces the old error by the new batch error.
The boundary $p=0$ recovers pure TSGD, while $p=1$ gives minibatch SGD.
For $0<p<1$, refreshing mitigates both persistent initialization error
and the accumulation of transport errors.

MARINA uses the same probabilistic choice in distributed optimization
\citep{gorbunov2021marina}. For $f=n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^n f_i$, its refresh
branch computes $H_{k+1}=\nabla f(x_{k+1})$, and its transport branch
uses compressed local gradient differences:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:marina-increment}
D_{k+1}^{\mathcal Q}
=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n
\mathcal Q_{i,k+1}\!\left(
\nabla f_i(x_{k+1})-\nabla f_i(x_k)\right).
\end{equation}
Unbiased compression makes this increment conditionally unbiased,
although the resulting gradient estimator need not be. Refreshing
removes accumulated compression error; transporting communicates
compressed changes. MARINA therefore illustrates the value of defining
TSGD through stochastic increments: transport noise can arise from
compression as well as stochastic gradient sampling.

\subsection{STORM and MVR: Mixing Fresh and Transported Estimates}
\label{subsec:mixing-estimates}

The momentum variance-reduction (MVR) recursion used by STORM mixes a
fresh estimate with a transported estimate at every iteration
\citep{cutkosky2019momentum}. Writing $\alpha\in[0,1]$ for the fresh
weight, its two equivalent forms are
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:mvr-mixture}
\begin{aligned}
g_{k+1}
&=(1-\alpha)(g_k+D_{k+1})+\alpha H_{k+1}\\
&=h(x_{k+1},\xi_{k+1})
 +(1-\alpha)\bigl(g_k-h(x_k,\xi_{k+1})\bigr).
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
Here $H_{k+1}$ and $D_{k+1}$ share the fresh sample $\xi_{k+1}$.
STORM uses an iteration-dependent mixing weight linked to its
stepsize; the fixed-weight notation exposes the common mechanism.

Substituting \eqref{eq:mvr-mixture} into
$e_{k+1}=g_{k+1}-\nabla f(x_{k+1})$ gives
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:mvr-error-recursion}
e_{k+1}=(1-\alpha)e_k+(1-\alpha)d_{k+1}
 +\alpha\bigl(H_{k+1}-\nabla f(x_{k+1})\bigr).
\end{equation}
Thus mixing damps the inherited error while introducing both transport
noise and fresh-gradient noise. A larger $\alpha$ strengthens the
correction but places more weight on pointwise noise; a smaller
$\alpha$ relies more on accurate gradient changes. The boundary
$\alpha=0$ is TSGD and $\alpha=1$ is SGD. Because the two new noises
share a sample, their covariance also enters the mean-square error;
their variances cannot generally be added as if they were independent.

\subsection{MARS: Transport Corrections within Momentum}
\label{subsec:mars-transport}

MARS scales the gradient-difference correction within momentum
\citep{yuan2025mars}. Before clipping, write $\beta=1-\beta_1$ for
the fresh-gradient weight, where $\beta_1$ is the original momentum
coefficient. Its estimator is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:mars-estimator}
g_{k+1}=(1-\beta)(g_k+\gamma D_{k+1})+\beta H_{k+1}.
\end{equation}
The difference $D_{k+1}$ uses the same sample at both endpoints.
Setting $\gamma=0$ gives the exponential moving average of MSGD;
$\gamma=1$ gives MVR with fresh weight $\beta$. For
$\gamma\in[0,1]$, the VENUS manuscript makes the interpolation explicit
\citep{venus2026draft}:
\[
g_{k+1}^{\mathrm{MARS}}
=(1-\gamma)g_{k+1}^{\mathrm{MSGD}}
 +\gamma g_{k+1}^{\mathrm{MVR}},
\]
where both candidate updates use the same incoming estimate, sample,
and fresh weight $\beta$. The boundary $\beta=0$, $\gamma=1$ of
\eqref{eq:mars-estimator} recovers pure transport.
For $\gamma\ne1$, the scaled increment $\gamma D_{k+1}$ has mean
$\gamma(\nabla f(x_{k+1})-\nabla f(x_k))$, so it generally does not
satisfy the unbiased-transport condition of
Assumption~\ref{ass:unbiased-transport}.

This relation identifies the transport component of MARS. Its full
implementations also use clipping and preconditioning, and the
cached-gradient approximation changes the sampling of the difference.
Those operations are not part of the pure TSGD recursion. In particular,
subtracting a cached gradient from a previous sample need not give a
conditionally unbiased increment satisfying our similarity assumption.

VENUS further separates the samples used by the MSGD and MVR branches
\citep{venus2026draft}. It retains a shared sample within each transported
difference but assigns part of the fresh-gradient contribution to an
independent batch. This changes the covariance between refreshing and
transporting, in addition to their weights. The connection highlights
that the combination of the two operations depends on how their samples
are coupled as well as how their outputs are mixed.

Refreshing supplies new information about the absolute gradient;
transporting exploits information about how that gradient changes.
These are complementary operations. Their combination can control the
two error sources of pure TSGD, with guarantees depending on the refresh
schedule, mixing weights, sampling, and iterate update. TSGD is therefore
a fundamental building block of modern stochastic optimization: its
recursion remains identifiable even when it is embedded in a more
elaborate algorithm.

\subsection{Why Combine Fundamental Estimators?}
\label{subsec:why-combine-estimators}

SGD, momentum SGD (MSGD), and TSGD embody three fundamental estimation
mechanisms: estimating gradients afresh, averaging past gradients,
and transporting an estimate through gradient changes. Each has a
characteristic limitation: SGD is exposed to pointwise noise, MSGD
can lag behind the changing gradient, and TSGD preserves initialization
errors and accumulates transport errors. Modern optimizers combine
these fundamental estimators through weighted averages, as in
MVR/STORM and MARS, or by alternating between fresh estimation and
transport, as in SARAH, SPIDER, and PAGE. These combinations have
three motivations.

\emph{Computational efficiency.} When individual stochastic gradients
are noisy, obtaining an accurate fresh estimate may require a large
batch. If their differences are much less noisy, a small batch can
suffice to estimate the gradient change accurately. Although each
coupled difference requires two gradient evaluations, the smaller
batch can still make a transport step substantially cheaper. SARAH,
SPIDER, and PAGE exploit this by reusing an accurate estimate through
many small-batch transport steps before recomputing it. This saves
work compared with forming a large-batch gradient at every iteration;
refreshing more often limits error accumulation but reduces that saving.

\emph{Complementary mechanisms.} Momentum averages noisy gradients,
but the information it retains comes from points the iterate has
already left. Transport updates this inherited information to account
for the change in the true gradient. It can therefore reduce momentum's
tracking lag while preserving the benefit of averaging across iterations.
Fresh gradients provide absolute information that pure transport cannot
recover, damping both initialization errors and accumulated transport
errors. Their weight controls the strength of this correction and the
amount of fresh noise introduced. MVR, STORM, and MARS combine these
roles through their mixing weights and transport corrections. The
mechanisms are complementary even within a fixed noise regime: each
addresses a limitation of the others.

\emph{Changing regimes.} Even for a fixed stochastic oracle, the
relative accuracy of fresh and transported estimates need not remain
constant during optimization. Under
Assumption~\ref{ass:centered-similarity}, the variance of a new
transport error is bounded by
$\delta^2\|x_{k+1}-x_k\|^2$ conditionally on the current history.
Shorter steps therefore tighten this bound, although they do not
remove errors already accumulated in the estimate.
Section~\ref{subsec:fixed-variance-variable-similarity} illustrates
how transport noise varies with the displacement even when pointwise
noise variance is constant. Conversely, fresh gradients may become
more reliable: in Section~\ref{subsec:multiplicative-noise-comparison},
their noise vanishes near the minimizer, while pure transport can
retain a nonzero tracking error. Thus the preferable balance can
change as the iterates move, steps shrink, and estimation errors
accumulate. Combining mechanisms gives an optimizer flexibility to
respond to these changes through its refresh schedule or mixing weights.

Mixing weights and refresh frequency determine how much an optimizer
relies on each mechanism. Fixed choices provide a compromise, while
adapting them during optimization can help respond to changing
conditions. Choosing this balance remains important: combining
estimators does not automatically improve convergence, and a poor
choice can perform worse than using one estimator alone.
